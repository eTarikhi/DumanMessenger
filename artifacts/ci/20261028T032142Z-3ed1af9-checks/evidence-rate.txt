Duman Messenger — what docs/04 §6's abuse limits cost and buy
Two caps, both off by default, both token buckets rather than per-minute tallies,
both enforced exactly where the document says and no further.

=== a compliant network loses nothing (docs/04 §6) ===
docs/04 §6 caps broadcasts at 5/min per node and relays at
30/min per source. The engine's own traffic generator sends
30 unicasts over 120 s — 15/min
network-wide, and each node beacons one OGM every 2 s. Neither is near either cap,
so the limits must be invisible. If they are not, they are not shippable.

  seed  scope    delivery   bcast reach   overhead   SUPER   bcast drops   relay drops
  42    super      100.0%         95.0%     952.9%    20.0%             0             0
  42    all        100.0%         95.0%     952.9%    20.0%             0             0
  7     super      100.0%         95.0%     566.3%    25.0%             0             0
  7     all        100.0%         95.0%     566.3%    25.0%             0             0
  11    super      100.0%         95.0%     647.7%    20.0%             0             0
  11    all        100.0%         95.0%     647.7%    20.0%             0             0

  OGMs are exempt from the relay cap, and this is where that decision shows: at 2 s a
  beacon a node emits 30/min, which is exactly the documented cap of
  30. Counting beacons would leave every node's own routing protocol holding
  the entire budget — measured in the test suite as 29 messages admitted in ten minutes
  instead of 300 — so the caps would fire on a completely honest network and the row
  above would not be zero.

=== verdict ===
  Zero drops on all 3 seeds under both enforcement scopes, and delivery is
  identical with the caps on and off. The limits cost a compliant network nothing.

=== what a 12× flooder costs, with and without the caps (docs/04 §6) ===
One node sends 60 broadcasts in 60 s — 60/min,
12× the documented cap — while 30 honest unicasts are in flight.
Four arms per seed: flood/no-flood × caps off/on. The delivery and overhead columns
are reported rather than asserted, because the answer is a measurement.

  The caps move honest delivery under flood by +0.0 points and
  overhead by +293.1 points. Neither number is asserted, for the reason docs/06
  gives for its seventeen rejected levers: a probe that only passes when the answer is the
  one its author hoped for is how a rejected lever gets published as a win.

  Read the suppressions column as a sum over enforcing nodes, not as messages: one flood
  broadcast heard by four SUPERs over its cap is four suppressions, which is why the mean
  (211) is larger than the 60 messages the source sent. And read the overhead
  column with its denominator in mind — it is transmissions per delivered message, and a
  suppressed flood copy is a transmission that never becomes a delivery, so capping a flood
  can raise the ratio while removing work. The transmissions column is the one that says
  what the radio actually did.

  §21.5's question, answered with a measurement rather than an opinion: docs/04 §6 says
  "SUPERs drop excess", so only SUPERs enforce, which bounds how *far* a flood travels
  and not whether the flooder's own neighbours see it — a NORMAL node neither relays far
  nor filters. Making every node enforce instead is the fifth arm above, and it is not a
  rule this document contains, so it ships as a knob (`broadcastCapScope`,
  `--broadcast-cap-scope super|all`) with the two measured against each other.

    reach    94.9% uncapped → 76.9% SUPERs only → 21.2% every node
    airtime  6084 → 4929 → 2304 transmissions

  The 8.3% that 5 admitted of 60 sent would imply is not reachable by either scope,
  because the honest broadcasts in the same window are counted in that reach too.

=== verdict ===
  The caps fired on all 3 seeds under a 12× flood and stayed silent without one,
  and the honest-network section shows they cost a compliant network nothing.

=== a local block, and who pays for it (docs/04 §6, bullet 1) ===
docs/04 §6 says "Block/mute peer locally (ignore msgs from pubKey)" and, in the same
section, "ignore floods". Those are two different reach: the first is about what a
user sees, the second about what a relay pays for. `blockScope` is the seam between
them — `delivery` (the default) filters the inbox only, `relay` refuses to forward
as well — and one node spamming 24 broadcasts is blocked at half the network
so the other half can show whether a block stays local.

    network delivery ratio              none 385.1% → delivery 398.7% → relay 401.3%
    transmissions                       none 3728 → delivery 3619 → relay 3268

  Read the second line as the price of "relay": 17.0 of the blocked
  sender's copies per seed never reached nodes that did not block it, because a blocker
  that refuses to forward is a hole in that source's routes. The first and third lines
  are the feature and its exemption. The second line is the locality claim — a block
  removes nothing from a device that did not block — though at 24 messages × 9
  bystanders it is a saturated one, so it rules out removal rather than proving the two
  worlds identical.

  The fourth line moves and the sixth moves with it, and neither is a cost of blocking.
  The sixth is a whole-network ratio whose numerator counts the blocked sender's own
  messages to the nodes that blocked it, so a working block reduces it by construction;
  reading it as "a block costs the network delivery" is the mistake this section exists
  to prevent. The fourth is unrelated traffic at unrelated nodes, and it moves because
  the fifth line's ACKs are transmissions: an ignored message is not acknowledged, a
  transmission not made is an RNG draw not spent, and jitter, the MPR wait and backoff
  are drawn per transmission — so the two worlds diverge at the first ignored message.
  The pruning half of that story is printed and is zero: docs/06 §20.5 measured
  gossip-pruning as essentially never firing, and it does not fire here either.

  Scope relay saves 352 transmissions per seed (9.7% of the network's
  airtime) that scope delivery spends forwarding someone the forwarder has blocked.
  Whether that trade is worth a hole in the blocked source's routes is a policy
  question, so both ship and the default is the one that cannot censor anybody.

PASS — the block filters what the blocker sees and nothing else: a bystander loses no
  copy of the blocked sender's traffic, an SOS still gets through under both scopes,
  and the network spends less airtime only when it is asked to.

PASS — the caps and the blocklist are inert on traffic that does not abuse them, fire on traffic that does, and neither reaches past the node that decided.
