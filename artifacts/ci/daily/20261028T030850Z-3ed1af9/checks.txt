=== 10. Contention window: the thing carrier sense was missing (docs/08 §12.13) ===
§12.10 measured carrier sense and found it made things worse, which is not what listening is
supposed to do. The reason is in the wake-up: every node waiting on a busy medium is woken at
the instant it clears, so they all find it free together and transmit together. Carrier sense
synchronised the herd instead of dispersing it. `channelBackoff: "binary"` draws a random
number of slots before taking a medium that is already free, and doubles the window it draws
from after a collision — the 802.11 answer, which §12.10 noted it had not modelled.

=== 11. Where the shedding happens (docs/08 §12.14) ===
§12.12 argued that the queue which overflows in a mesh belongs to a relay, and §12.13 found
that queue drops outnumber receiver collisions about eight to one under carrier sense. Both
were read off totals. This attributes them: who sheds, what they shed, and — the question
that decides whether congestion-aware forwarding is even available — whether the shedding
relay had a peer in range at least as close to the destination as the next hop it chose. If
it did, the packet could have gone round the congestion. If it did not, the only mechanism
left is to stop generating the packet, which means propagating pressure to the source.
transmitter  receiver   media  drops   at a relay   file/voice   alternative existed   file
halfDuplex   first      no       176       80 %           0 %                9 %          NaN min
halfDuplex   first      yes     1441       15 %          82 %                3 %          3.28 min
csma         first      no       563       83 %           0 %                5 %          NaN min
csma         first      yes     2995       18 %          79 %                9 %          3.64 min

Read the third column first: if the shed packets are mostly file or voice chunks, the thing
generating the pressure is a transfer window, and the fix that reaches it is pressure
propagated to the source. The fourth column is the fork in the road — a high share means
congestion-aware forwarding has somewhere to put the packet, and a low one means it does
not, so rerouting around a saturated relay is not a mechanism this topology offers.

--- the one-peer case, which is where a sender can throttle itself ---
Two nodes, one hop, a 1 MB file through a window of 8 into a queue 4 deep, so the transfer
is committed twice as far ahead as the radio can hold. This is the case §12.12 measured the
flag on, re-measured here because the mechanism was wrong when it was: the window was floored
at one, so a throttled sender still handed a packet to a full queue and had it shed, and the
stall path bypassed the window altogether. A window that can reach zero, a stall path that
consults the same gate, and a resume when the radio drains are what a socket does.

=== 12. The capture effect: bracketing the receiver instead of guessing it (docs/08 §12.15) ===
§12.9 gave the receiver two models and neither is a real radio. `none` takes everything
however busy it is, which is optimistic; `first` locks onto whichever packet arrived first and
never lets a strong one survive a weak one, which is pessimistic. Real receivers do the third
thing: they demodulate the stronger signal *through* the weaker one when the margin is enough.
`strongest` models that, and the point of measuring it is not to pick a winner but to turn a
point estimate into a bracket — `first` bounds the loss from above, `none` from below, and a
field test lands somewhere between.

=== 14. Occupancy: what a radio is busy for is not how long a packet takes to arrive ===
The shipped model charges every packet a calibrated hop latency — 120 ms on BLE, which
serializationMs's own comment says already includes one full-sized GATT write — plus the
excess over 1 024 B. That is right at 1 024 B and wrong below it, so a short text pays
what a media chunk pays, and a beacon paid it too while having no size at all.

Beacons now have one, derived from the fields docs/03 §4 says an OGM carries: fixed
  46 B + 32 B per advertised neighbour + 33 B per route entry, bounded by the
  advertise limits the engine enforces (30 neighbours, 8 routes).
  A typical beacon (10 neighbours, 8 routes) is 630 B = 63.0 ms of airtime, about half the flat
  120 ms it was charged. A fully loaded one is 1270 B, which is over the 1024 B BLE ceiling —
  so a dense node's beacon would ride Wi-Fi Direct. Nothing sized beacons before, so
  nothing noticed. docs/06 §24.6.

  mean of 3 seeds, 30 messages injected over 60 s after a 90 s settle

  campus_quad      free         hop-latency           4.23x     84.60 min     100.0%             0           0
  campus_quad      free         airtime               1.92x     38.46 min     100.0%             0           0

  campus_quad      csma         hop-latency           4.16x     83.22 min      98.7%           507        3886
  campus_quad      csma         airtime               1.88x     37.66 min     100.0%           361        2067

  Three things this says, and one it does not.

  Sizing packets properly roughly halves the demanded radio time — campus_quad 3.9x to
  1.7x, festival_crowd 8.4x to 5.8x — and it does not make the demand physical. Above 1
  the model asks for more airtime than exists, so the over-subscription docs/06 §24.3
  measured is only partly the flat per-packet charge: the dominant term is the *volume*
  of beacon receptions and the gossip that follows them, which a per-packet correction
  cannot touch. At 60 nodes every node beaconing every 2 s is a real protocol finding
  about docs/03 §1's interval, not a simulator artefact.

  Under the shipped free/none baseline the two models are the same world: nothing reads
  occupancy there except the wake-lock accounting, so delivery, latency, overhead and
  every count are bit-identical and only the telemetry moves. radio-occupancy.test.ts
  asserts that field by field, which is what makes the knob safe to ship defaulted off.

  Where contention *is* modelled the two diverge, and §12's published numbers were
  measured under the flat charge — but not uniformly in one direction, which is the part
  worth not smoothing over. Under csma, sizing packets properly cuts crowd deferrals by
  about half (13 540 to 6 382) and lifts crowd delivery 71.5% to 81.9%, and on
  campus_quad it takes delivery from 98.7% to 100% with 29% fewer queue drops. Under
  half-duplex in the same crowd it goes the other way: delivery 77.9% to 75.9% and
  deferrals up 4%. A radio that frees sooner transmits sooner, and whether that helps
  depends on whether the model lets it hear anyone first — csma does, half-duplex does
  not. This probe reports the disagreement and does not claim to have established why.
  What follows is that §12's contention magnitudes are a property of the occupancy
  model they were measured under, not of the mesh.

  What this does not say: that the flat model is wrong about latency. Delivery still
  takes a hop latency per hop under both models, because that is what docs/02 §6's budget
  measures and it is unchanged here. Only the busy-radio duration moved.


=== 15. The beacon interval: docs/03 says 2 s and nothing in the corpus says why ===
§24.6 found the control plane alone asks for several times one radio's airtime at 60
nodes, priced correctly. The interval is the lever that follows, and it has a hard
ceiling: neighbour scores expire at NEIGHBOR_SCORE_TTL_MS 30 s, so an interval near
that leaves one missed beacon — and BLE links lose packets routinely — long enough to
drop a neighbour that is still there. Priced under radioOccupancy: airtime, because
the question is capacity and the flat charge would put the same artefact in every arm.

  rows and only the demand moves. The two levers are not interchangeable: an alias
  shortens every beacon in a dense world without making any route staler, while a longer
  interval thins them and trades freshness for airtime. Which one a deployment should
  pull first is answered by the two tables above rather than argued here.

  What neither tests: an interval that *adapts* to density, which is what the two
  scenario tables together argue for and which docs/03 §1 does not propose. Making the
  beacon interval a function of peer count is a routing change with its own convergence
  question — a node that slows its beacons because it has many neighbours is exactly the
  node its neighbours most need to hear from — so it is named here as the follow-up
  rather than swept. docs/06 §24.7.


=== 16. Why a 60-node crowd has two modes, and the two explanations that failed ===
§24.8 found `festival_crowd` bimodal: broadcast reach lands either near 98% or between
50 and 79%, and which seeds are weak differs per arm. Two explanations are obvious, and
both are testable here — one from the radio graph, one from the forwarding policy.

  Hypothesis 1: the crowd partitions, so a broadcast cannot reach everyone.

  interval   seed     reach   components (largest first)

  Partly true and not the story. At 2 s two of eight seeds split 40/20, and both are
  weak-reach seeds — so for those the ceiling is the component the sender is in, not a
  routing failure. But a weak seed at 2 s (2024, 79%) is fully connected, and at 12 s
  **all eight worlds are connected** while reach still spans 55-98%. Partitioning
  explains two rows of sixteen and cannot explain the mode.

  Hypothesis 2: stale two-hop views make MPR suppress the only path.

  interval   policy      reach   delivery   MPR suppressed   overhead   per-seed reach

  Refuted. `flood-all` suppresses nothing at all and spends roughly six times the
  overhead, and the weak mode survives it: per-seed reach under flood-all still reads
  42%, 55% and 66% at 2 s, where MPR gave 79%, 54% and 87%. At 2 s flooding is
  *worse* on the mean (81.8% against 86.5%) despite unlimited retransmission. A
  forwarding policy that cannot be improved by removing the forwarding policy is not
  what is limiting reach.

  Hypothesis 3: the graph measured at the *end* of the window is not the graph the
  broadcasts had to cross. Sampled 6 times inside it, 10 s apart.

  Confirmed, and it reverses the verdict on hypothesis 1. Reach tracks the connectivity
  *inside* the window, not the snapshot after it: every seed connected for all six
  samples reaches 98%, and every seed that was split for any of them lands between 54%
  and 93%, in proportion to how much of the window was split. At 12 s seed 2024 is
  connected for 17% of samples with a mean largest component of 72% — and reaches 55%,
  while the end-of-window table reports it as fully connected, because the crowd had
  re-joined by then. So the two modes are one phenomenon: 60 mobile nodes in this area
  split and re-join repeatedly, a broadcast sent while the graph is split cannot reach
  the other side, and reach averaged over the window lands wherever the split left it.

  Hypothesis 1 was not wrong about the mechanism, only about when to look for it, and
  the end-of-window component table it used cannot see a partition that has healed.
  Hypothesis 2 stays refuted: flooding does not help, because a broadcast cannot cross a
  gap that exists at the moment it is sent, however many copies are made.

  What this leaves untested, and worth naming: a receiver asleep inside its duty-cycle
  window when the broadcast passes, which scansSkippedAsleep would quantify per seed, and
  queue shedding under the crowd's own load, which §12.14 measured as mostly the source's
  own. Neither is needed to explain the bimodality now. docs/06 §24.10.

  One thing that fell out and is worth keeping: MPR's overhead against flood-all in the
  densest scenario, on eight seeds, is 2 319% against 17 728% at 2 s and 2 662% against
  14 857% at 12 s — **13% and 18% of flood-all**, against skills/02's acceptance target
  of ≤70%, while delivering *more* reach at 2 s. That target is met here by a wide
  margin, and this is the first place it has been measured in a 60-node world.


=== 17. Deriving the beacon interval from density: the follow-up §24.7 argued for ===
§24.7 found no single interval serving both densities: at 20 nodes stretching 2 s to 25 s
costs nothing and cuts demanded radio time tenfold, while at 60 nodes 5 s beats the
specified 2 s and everything longer degrades delivery. The obvious follow-up is to derive
the interval per node instead of choosing one, stretching it in proportion to peer count
so the receptions a node imposes stay constant, anchored so ten peers reproduces the
specified 2 s. Eight seeds, three scenarios, priced under radioOccupancy: airtime.

  Read the last line of each pair before the rest, because it decides whether the pair
  means anything. §24.10 showed reach tracks the largest in-window component, so two
  arms whose connectivity differs are not a comparison of what was changed.

  sparse_village and campus_quad are comparable — both modes land in the same topology
  — and in both the derived interval buys no delivery and costs airtime: 3.1x the
  demanded radio time for 1.6 points in the village, 1.2x for a loss of 0.7 on campus.
  The mechanism is the clamp's floor: a node with three peers beacons every 500 ms,
  four times the specified rate, to hold a reception load nobody was struggling with.

  festival_crowd is where the derived interval saves airtime, 5.7x down to 3.3x, and it
  is exactly where the comparison cannot be read: the two modes land in different
  topologies (mean largest component 94% against 85%), so the 8.6-point delivery gap
  cannot be attributed to the interval. That is not a defence of the lever — it is the
  reason the verdict rests on the two scenarios where the topology is comparable.

  The fourth pair is the one that settles it, and it was added after the first three.
  With mobility off the two arms share a topology by construction — same connected share,
  same mean largest component, and the same per seed, not only on average — so the
  comparison isolates the interval instead of measuring which mode each seed landed in.
  There the derived interval takes 44% off the demanded radio time and 44% off the
  beacons, which is what the rule does and so does not depend on any seed. Read the
  per-seed delivery column before believing anything else about it: better on four
  seeds, worse on three, level on one, with most of the +2.5-point mean coming from one
  seed going 45 to 67. Reach is the stronger claim, equal or better in seven of eight.
  So the lever is cheaper and does not cost reach, and it does not buy delivery; the
  mobile crowd cannot say anything at all, because changing the beacon schedule diverges
  a bimodal world and eight seeds cannot resolve that.

  What ships is still fixed, and now for a more precise reason than §24.11 gave: the
  lever is shown to help where the topology can be held equal and shown to be unreadable
  where it cannot, and the shipped condition is the one where it cannot. Turning it on
  would mean changing docs/03 §4's 2 s on evidence from a static network. docs/06
  §24.11-§24.12.

  And the claimed benefit is not measurable in this engine. Look at the battery columns:
  cutting scans by 70% in sparse_village changes NORMAL drain from 1.331 to 1.331 %/h and
  SUPER from 1.875 to 1.875 — identical to three decimals — because `batteryDrain` charges
  the duty-cycle *window* (`isAwake`, `sleepDrainFactor`) and not the scan events inside
  it. Everywhere else the battery moves by 2% or less and in both directions, which is
  noise. So 'reduces redundant wake-ups' is true of the wake-up count and invisible to
  only currency §1.2 states its impact in. This is the third time this document has hit the
  same wall: §24.1 found no cost for the Wi-Fi Direct group-owner role, §24.3 found no
  per-packet term, and here there is no per-scan term. The battery model is a role-based
  hourly rate, and a mitigation that works by doing less of something cannot be priced by
  it. Saying so is the finding; inventing a millijoule-per-scan constant to make the row
  evaluate would be measuring a guess.

  little to discover either way. That inverts §1.2's framing for the dense case — there,
  'dense = shorter interval' is a delivery lever with a battery cost the engine cannot
  charge, not a saving. Whether the trade is worth making needs a device, and §1.2's own
  mitigation table is where that question belongs. docs/06 §24.15.

=== packet size, padding and the transport boundary (docs/07 §4) ===
skills/03's packet is header 27 B + nonce 12 B + tag 16 B + from 32 B + ts 8 B = 95 B,
padded to 256 B blocks, so the wire size is ceil((95 + body) / 256) × 256. BLE carries up to
1024 B. The heuristic this replaced was 64 + 4 B per character, which crossed the BLE ceiling
at 241 characters instead of 930 — and transport is what sets per-hop latency in this model.
